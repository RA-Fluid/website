How Pipe Diameter Reshapes Vertical Gas-Liquid Flow

Published Research: M. R. Ansari and R. Azadi, “Effect of diameter and axial location on upward gas-liquid two-phase flow patterns in intermediate-scale vertical tubes,” Annals of Nuclear Energy, 94, 530–540, 2016.

Gas and liquid flowing together through vertical pipes can organise into bubbly, slug, churn, and annular regimes, each producing different pressure fluctuations, transport behaviour, and operational risks. During my MSc research, I helped design, manufacture, assemble, and commission a multifunctional air-water facility to investigate how these regimes change with pipe diameter and distance from the inlet.

The facility supported co-current and counter-current operation, upward and downward flow, and interchangeable transparent test sections. This study examined co-current upward flow through 3.4 m acrylic tubes with internal diameters of 40 and 70 mm. Air and water were supplied through independently controlled circulation systems, combined in a configurable mixing chamber, and separated downstream for recirculation.

Two high-speed cameras recorded the flow simultaneously at axial locations of 1.73 and 3.22 m. Diffuse backlighting improved interface contrast, while image processing was used to isolate the gas structures and classify both the principal flow regimes and finer sub-patterns.

Key Result: Increasing the tube diameter from 40 to 70 mm did not substantially shift the bubbly-to-slug transition, but it sharply contracted the slug-flow region, expanded churn flow, and revealed a distinct semi-annular regime.

The Engineering Challenge

The apparatus needed to maintain controlled air and water delivery across a broad flow range while allowing simultaneous observation at multiple downstream locations and rapid exchange of pipe diameters. The resulting facility integrated compression, temperature conditioning, constant-head water circulation, bubble removal, configurable phase mixing, optical access, and multi-stage downstream separation.

Measurement Principle

Air and water superficial velocities are varied systematically while high-speed cameras record the evolving interface at two axial locations. Image-based classification identifies the dominant flow pattern and maps the transition boundaries among bubbly, slug, churn, semi-annular, and annular regimes.

Key System Features

  • 40 and 70 mm transparent acrylic test sections.
  • 3.4 m vertical observation length.
  • Configurable air-water mixing chamber with temperature conditioning.
  • Constant-head water supply and multi-stage downstream phase separation.
  • Simultaneous high-speed imaging at two axial locations with diffuse backlighting.
  • > 180 conditions studied in the 40 mm tube.
  • > 300 conditions studied in the 70 mm tube.

Principal Observations

The extensive mapping of air and water superficial velocities across different tube diameters and axial locations yielded several critical insights into flow pattern transitions:

1. Flow Regime Mapping in the 40 mm Tube

The 40 mm tube exhibited bubbly, slug, churn, and annular flow. Image analysis separated bubbly flow into dispersed, agitated, and agglomerated sub-patterns and divided slug flow into small and large slug structures according to the Taylor-bubble length.

2. Diameter Expansion and the Semi-Annular Regime

Increasing the tube diameter to 70 mm substantially reduced the stable slug-flow region and shifted more conditions toward churn flow. The larger tube also exhibited a distinct semi-annular regime, characterised by wall-liquid patches that repeatedly merged, thickened, fell downward, ruptured, and were subsequently carried upward again by the gas stream.

3. Axial Location Independence

Changing the observation location from 1.73 to 3.22 m produced little change in the measured transition boundaries for the investigated conditions. Tube diameter was therefore considerably more influential than axial location over the measured range.
