VOSET: Combining Mass Conservation with Accurate Interface Geometry

Published Research: R. Azadi, [Co-Authors], "VOSET: A Coupled Volume-of-Fluid and Level-Set Method," [Journal Name], [Year].

Moving gas-liquid interfaces are among the most difficult features to simulate reliably. The numerical method must conserve the volume of each phase while accurately resolving curvature, surface tension, deformation, breakup, and changes in topology.

Two established approaches offer complementary strengths: Volume-of-fluid methods conserve mass effectively but represent the interface through a discontinuous volume-fraction field, making accurate curvature calculations difficult. Level-set methods provide a smooth signed-distance function, but repeated advection and reinitialisation introduce mass loss. During my MSc research, I developed a finite-volume two-phase solver incorporating a modified volume-of-fluid and level-set method (VOSET). The approach reconstructs a local signed-distance field geometrically from the mass-conserving volume fraction, combining the principal advantages of both formulations without separately solving the conventional level-set advection and re-distancing equations.

Volume-fraction advection → Interface reconstruction → Near-interface flagging → Geometrical distance calculation → Curvature & surface tension → Flow solution

The Numerical Problem

A volume-of-fluid (VOF) method stores the fraction of one phase contained within every computational cell. Because it operates directly on phase volume, it offers strong mass conservation. However, the volume-fraction field changes abruptly across the interface. Surface normals and curvature obtained directly from this discontinuous field contain significant numerical error, which can generate artificial "parasitic" currents even around a theoretically stationary bubble.

A level-set method represents the interface as the zero contour of a continuous signed-distance function, providing smoother normals and curvature. However, maintaining the distance-function property requires repeated reinitialisation, an operation that can displace the interface and violate phase-volume conservation. VOSET bridges these methods by retaining VOF advection while geometrically reconstructing the level-set information only where it is needed near the interface.

The Numerical Challenge

Improving interface geometry must not compromise phase-volume conservation or make the solver prohibitively expensive. VOSET solves this by reconstructing a smooth distance field only within a narrow band around the interface. This provides the geometric information needed for capillary forces while retaining geometrical volume-of-fluid advection as the mass-conserving foundation.

Computational Principle

Advect the interface through a mass-conserving volume-fraction field, reconstruct a local signed-distance function geometrically around the interface, and use that continuous representation to calculate normals, curvature, surface tension, and material properties more accurately. This avoids transporting two separate interface fields and eliminates the conventional level-set reinitialisation equation entirely.

The Method at a Glance

  • Mass loss of order 10-8: Tested in demanding oblate-disk benchmark cases.
  • < 0.8% Pressure Error: Captured static Young-Laplace pressure jump accurately.
  • 59% Reduction: In parasitic-current magnitude through targeted mesh refinement.
  • 0.072% Velocity Difference: Between the two finest tested bubble-rise meshes.
  • 8 Validated Regimes: Full morphological spectrum of rising bubbles.
  • Autonomous Topology Handling: Automatic treatment of filament stretching, breakup, and coalescence.

How the Solver Works

The method follows a repeating sequence designed to optimize computational cost without sacrificing geometric fidelity:

1. Volume-of-Fluid Advection

The interface is transported through a piecewise-linear interface-calculation (PLIC) formulation based on Young’s geometrical VOF method. Each mixed cell contains a reconstructed line segment representing the local phase boundary, and the phase flux through each face is calculated geometrically. (During implementation, corrected geometrical relations were derived for several previously published flux expressions).

2. Near-Interface Identification

Only cells close to the interface require a signed-distance value. A local 7 × 7 stencil is used to identify a sufficiently wide band around every interfacial cell while avoiding the cost of reconstructing the distance function throughout the entire domain. Duplicate cells shared by overlapping stencils are removed.

3. Geometrical Distance Reconstruction

The linear interface segment within each VOF cell provides known start and end coordinates. For every flagged cell, the minimum distance to nearby interface segments is calculated geometrically. The sign is assigned according to the local phase, producing a field that is negative in one phase, positive in the other, and exactly zero at the interface.

4. Interface Refinement

The reconstructed distance field provides smoother estimates of interface normals and curvature. These improved normals are returned to the geometrical VOF reconstruction, repeating iteratively to obtain an optimal representation of the gas-liquid boundary.

5. One-Fluid Flow Solution

Both fluids are represented through one set of incompressible mass and momentum equations. Density and viscosity vary smoothly across a finite interface thickness using a regularised Heaviside function. Surface tension is applied through the continuum-surface-force model, and pressure-velocity is solved using a finite-volume SIMPLE formulation.

Validation Strategy & Outcomes

The solver was tested progressively, beginning with isolated capillary effects and advancing to highly deformable rising bubbles.

Parasitic Current Assessment & Static Bubbles

A theoretically stationary circular bubble should produce no flow. The VOSET implementation yielded a static Young-Laplace pressure difference error below 0.8%, and generated lower maximum spurious velocity (parasitic currents) than standard coupled level-set/VOF results over the relevant interface-thickness range. Furthermore, surface-tension-driven relaxation successfully smoothed an initially square bubble toward its stable circular shape without gravity.

Capturing Eight Bubble Regimes

The validated solver accurately simulated eight established regimes of gas bubbles rising through quiescent viscous liquids: spherical, oblate ellipsoidal, oblate ellipsoidal disk, oblate ellipsoidal cap, spherical cap with closed wake, spherical cap with open wake, smooth skirted, and wavy skirted. Predicted terminal shapes and velocities closely matched the classical experiments of Bhaga and Weber.

Topological Change Without Manual Intervention

In demanding skirted bubble regimes, thin gas filaments developed behind the main bubble, stretched under flow, and broke into smaller daughter bubbles that subsequently coalesced. The Eulerian VOSET framework captured this progressive filament stretching, rupture, oscillation, and wake asymmetry automatically within a fixed mesh, without explicitly reconnecting or remeshing the interface.

Does Initial Bubble Shape Matter?

An additional study tested spherical, horizontally elliptical, and vertically elliptical initial bubbles of equal area. While initial geometries produced distinct short-time accelerations, velocity histories converged after approximately 0.3 s, and all bubbles evolved toward the same terminal oblate-disk shape—proving the final state was governed entirely by fluid properties and force balance rather than assumed initial conditions.
